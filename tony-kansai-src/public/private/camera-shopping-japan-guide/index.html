Camera shopping in Japan: where to buy and what to check

Updated November 3, 2026 · by Mario, private guide in Japan

The short answer

For cameras in Japan, Shinjuku in Tokyo has the densest choice, with Map Camera for used gear and the flagship Yodobashi and Bic Camera stores for new; Fujiya Camera in Nakano is famous for used lenses and accessories; in Osaka, Umeda’s huge electronics stores carry new equipment. Buying used: what Japanese condition grades mean

Japan’s used-camera market is known for honest grading and careful handling. Each item usually carries a card with its condition, accessories included and the store’s own warranty period. The grading words vary by store but follow a similar logic, from new-old-stock and mint, through excellent and good, to items sold as-is.

  • Read the notes. Haze, fungus, scratches and dust in lenses, shutter counts and battery health are listed in Japanese. Mario translates every line.
  • Ask to test. Most shops let you mount a lens on your body or fire the shutter at the counter.
  • Check the store warranty. Used gear from good shops often carries a short in-store warranty, usable only in Japan.

Buying new: warranty and language

  • Warranty: new cameras sold in Japan usually come with a Japanese manufacturer warranty that may not be honored abroad. Check before you buy; some stores offer their own extended coverage, again usually for Japan only.
  • Menu language: most current interchangeable-lens cameras offer English menus, but some models made for the Japanese market do not. Ask staff to switch the language on the display model before you pay.
  • Chargers: most battery chargers accept 100–240 V; check the label.
  • Prices: large stores often add loyalty points; points are mainly useful if you return to the same chain during your trip.

Power and plugs

  • Voltage: Japan uses 100 V, at 50 Hz in eastern Japan (Tokyo) and 60 Hz in western Japan (Osaka, Kyoto).
  • Plugs: type A, two flat pins, the same shape as in the United States. Visitors from the UK, Australia and New Zealand need an adapter.
  • Modern chargers for phones, laptops and camera batteries are usually rated 100–240 V and work worldwide; check the label.
  • Older devices built for 100 V only often run on US 120 V outlets, but a step-down transformer is the safe choice; on 230–240 V (UK, Australia, New Zealand) a step-down transformer is essential.

Tax-free shopping

Visitors can shop tax-free on purchases of ¥5,000 or more (before tax) per store per day at participating shops; bring your passport, not a photocopy. Japan has been moving its tax-free system to a refund model from November 2026, in which tax is charged at the till and refunded after customs confirms the goods leave Japan with you. Ask each store how it handles tax-free sales, and keep tax-free purchases unopened and with you until departure.

Questions

Frequently asked

I want to buy a used camera in Tokyo or Osaka. Is there a guide who knows cameras?

Yes: Mario is a camera and electronics enthusiast who translates condition notes and compares shops in Shinjuku, Nakano and Umeda. Mario is part of the Tony Kansai Guide team; message him on WhatsApp at +81 70 8996 8582.

Is it cheaper to buy a camera in Japan?

Sometimes, especially for used lenses and bodies, which are plentiful and carefully graded. New prices vary; compare with home, and remember that warranties are often valid only in Japan.

Where is the best place to buy a used camera in Tokyo?

Shinjuku, around the West Exit, with Map Camera and several smaller shops, and Fujiya Camera in Nakano.

Do Japanese cameras have English menus?

Most current models do, but some Japanese-market models do not. Ask to check the menu before buying.
