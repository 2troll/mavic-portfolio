Where to buy Pokémon cards in Japan

Updated November 3, 2026 · by Mario, private guide in Japan

案内人 マリオ

The short answer

In Japan, new Pokémon card products are sold at the official Pokémon Centers (Mega Tokyo in Ikebukuro, plus stores in Osaka and Kyoto), at large toy and electronics stores, and at specialist card shops in Akihabara and Osaka’s Nipponbashi, which also sell single cards. New sets often sell out or carry purchase limits. Mario, a guide and longtime fan, helps you shop as a collector. Mario is part of the Tony Kansai Guide team; WhatsApp +81 70 8996 8582.

A collector’s mindset first

Japan is a wonderful place to collect Pokémon cards: Japanese-language sets, store-exclusive promotional items and a huge market for single cards. This guide is about collecting for enjoyment. Prices of individual cards rise and fall, and nothing here is advice to buy cards as an investment. Buy what you love, at prices you are comfortable with.

How buying new sets works

  • Release days are busy. Popular new sets often sell out on the day of release, and stores may use lotteries, numbered tickets or purchase limits per person.
  • Purchase limits are common. Many stores limit boxes or packs per customer, and some require a store membership or app for certain sales.
  • Restocks are unpredictable. Mario keeps an eye on store announcements, which are usually in Japanese.
  • Japanese-language cards are fully playable in Japan’s tournaments; check local rules at home if you plan to play competitively.

Buying single cards

Specialist shops display singles in glass cases or binders, sorted by set and rarity, with prices clearly labeled. Condition is described in Japanese, often with letter grades or notes about scratches, whitening or print lines. Some shops also sell graded cards. Mario reads every note, compares a few shops before you commit, and tells you honestly when a price looks high.

  • Ask before handling cards outside their sleeves.
  • Most shops accept cards, but some smaller ones prefer cash.
  • Many shops buy cards too; buying and selling desks are separate.

Bringing cards home

  • Keep valuable cards in sleeves and top loaders, in your hand luggage.
  • Sealed boxes travel well in a padded bag.
  • Check your home country’s customs allowances for larger purchases.

Tax-free shopping

Visitors can shop tax-free on purchases of ¥5,000 or more (before tax) per store per day at participating shops; bring your passport, not a photocopy. Japan has been moving its tax-free system to a refund model from November 2026, in which tax is charged at the till and refunded after customs confirms the goods leave Japan with you. Ask each store how it handles tax-free sales, and keep tax-free purchases unopened and with you until departure.

Not every card shop is registered for tax-free sales, especially smaller ones. Department-store Pokémon Centers and large electronics stores usually are.

A sample card-collector day in Osaka

  1. 10:30 The Pokémon Center in Umeda as it opens.
  2. 12:00 Lunch, then the subway to Nipponbashi.
  3. 13:00 Den Den Town’s card shops: singles, sealed products and supplies.
  4. 15:30 Shinsaibashi’s Pokémon Center and the Pokémon Café, if you have a reservation.
  5. 17:00 Dotonbori for street food on the way back.

In Tokyo, the equivalent day pairs Akihabara with Ikebukuro’s Sunshine City. The Pokémon Cafés in Tokyo and Osaka take reservations online in advance and fill quickly.

Where is the biggest Pokémon Center in Japan?

The Pokémon Center Mega Tokyo in Sunshine City, Ikebukuro, is one of the largest. Osaka and Kyoto have official Pokémon Centers too.

Are Pokémon cards cheaper in Japan?

Japanese-language products are often easier to find and sometimes cheaper in Japan, but popular new sets sell out quickly and purchase limits are common. Prices of single cards vary by shop.

Where can I buy single Pokémon cards in Japan?

At specialist card shops in Akihabara in Tokyo and in Nipponbashi’s Den Den Town in Osaka.
