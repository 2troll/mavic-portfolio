How to tell a waiter in Japanese what you can’t eat

Tick what you can’t eat: halal, no pork, no alcohol, vegetarian, vegan or allergies. Press “Show the waiter” and a full-screen card appears in polite Japanese, with an English line under each sentence and a button so your phone reads it aloud. It’s free and there’s nothing to sign up for.

How to use it

  1. Tick what you can’t eat. If you eat halal, you don’t need to tick “no pork” or “no alcohol”: they’re already included.
  2. Check the preview: every Japanese sentence has its translation underneath, so you know exactly what you’re showing.
  3. At the restaurant, press “Show the waiter” and turn the phone towards them. If your phone has a Japanese voice, “Read aloud” says it for you.
  4. If they say they don’t know or can’t guarantee it, believe them: order something else or find another place.

What really happens in Japanese kitchens

In Japan almost nobody will serve you a dish with something you said you don’t eat. The problem is what you can’t see: stocks, sauces and fats inside dishes that look harmless.

Dashi contains fish

The base stock of Japanese cooking is almost always made with dried bonito (katsuobushi, かつおぶし) and sometimes dried sardines (niboshi). It goes into miso soup, udon and soba broth, many sauces and Japanese rolled omelette. A vegetable dish may not be vegetarian because of the stock. Dashi made only with kombu seaweed and shiitake mushrooms is: ask 「だしは何を使っていますか？」.

Soy sauce and many sauces contain alcohol

Brewed soy sauce has a little alcohol from the fermentation itself, and Japanese cooking uses a lot of mirin (a sweet rice seasoning that contains alcohol) and cooking sake (料理酒): in teriyaki, sukiyaki, simmered dishes (nimono) and marinades. If you take no alcohol at all, say so clearly: the card asks specifically for no mirin and no cooking sake.

Tonkotsu means pork

Tonkotsu ramen (豚骨) is pork-bone broth, and many other ramen mix pork or chicken into the broth and come topped with chashu (roast pork). A “chicken” ramen can still contain pork fat. Look for halal-certified ramen or vegan ramen.

Lard (ラード) and gelatin (ゼラチン)

Lard is used in gyoza, fried rice (chahan) and some breads and fried foods. The gelatin in desserts, gummies and mousses is often pork, and the label rarely says which animal it comes from. In convenience stores (konbini), look on the label for 豚 (pork), ポーク (pork), ラード and ゼラチン.

Many kitchens can’t guarantee anything

Small kitchens share oil, griddles and utensils, and many sauces arrive ready-made from a factory. A kind waiter may tell you it’s fine without really knowing. If the answer is 「わかりません」 (I don’t know) or 「保証できません」 (we can’t guarantee it), that is an honest answer: take it seriously.

The safe option: halal-certified restaurants

If you eat strictly halal, the safe option is a halal-certified restaurant, and asking whether the certificate is current. In Kyoto there are restaurant categories supervised by Muslim bodies in the city, such as the Kyoto Muslim Association (KMA), including “Halal”, “Muslim Friendly” and “Pork-Free”. If you are vegetarian or vegan, look for shōjin ryōri (精進料理), Buddhist temple cooking based on vegetables, tofu and seaweed, or restaurants with a vegan menu, and still ask about the dashi.

Allergies: what to know

Soy sauce contains wheat and soy; ごま (sesame) and sesame oil are in many dressings, and shellfish turns up in stocks and in shrimp rice crackers (えびせんべい). On packaged food in Japan the main allergens (shrimp, crab, walnut, wheat, buckwheat or soba, egg, milk and peanut) must be on the label, but a restaurant is not required to tell you. If your allergy is severe, carry your medication and a doctor’s letter. In Japan, the ambulance number is 119.

Frequently asked questions

How do you say “halal” in Japanese?

It is written ハラール (harāru) and is more and more widely understood in Japan. To leave no doubt, the card also says you don’t eat pork or anything from pork, nothing with alcohol (no mirin or cooking sake either) and that meat must be halal-certified.

How do I say I’m vegetarian in Japanese?

「ベジタリアンです。肉と魚は食べられません。」 (Bejitarian desu. Niku to sakana wa taberaremasen). Always add the stock sentence, 「かつおだしや鶏ガラスープなど、肉や魚のだしも食べられません。」, because bonito dashi is in almost everything.

Is soy sauce halal?

Brewed soy sauce has a little alcohol from the fermentation itself, and many recipes add mirin or sake. Halal-certified soy sauce exists, but in an ordinary restaurant you won’t know which one they use. If you eat strictly halal, ask or choose a certified restaurant.

Does ramen contain pork?

Tonkotsu (豚骨) is pork-bone broth, and many ramen come with chashu (pork) on top or pork fat in the broth, even when the menu doesn’t say so. Look for halal-certified or vegan ramen.

Can I trust the waiter if they say it’s fine?

The card is a help, not a guarantee: it explains clearly what you need and the kitchen decides whether it can do it. Factory sauces and shared oil can slip past the waiter. For halal, the safe option is a restaurant with a current certificate.

Does it work for a severe allergy?

Tick “My allergy is severe” and the card says even a small amount can be life-threatening and that cooking with the same oil or utensils is dangerous too. Still carry your medication and a doctor’s letter; if something happens, call 119, the ambulance number in Japan.

Does it work offline?

You need to load the page once. If you’re worried about losing signal in the restaurant, take a screenshot of the card or copy the Japanese text into your notes beforehand.

Are my choices saved?

Yes, only in your phone’s browser, for the next meal. Nothing is sent anywhere. “Clear all” removes them.

Will Tony help me eat halal or vegetarian on the tour?

Yes: on a tour with Tony you choose together where to eat and he helps you ask at the restaurant. Meals are paid separately from the guide.
