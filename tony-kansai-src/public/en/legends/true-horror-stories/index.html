Cases and legends with a documented basis

Each case keeps three things apart: what happened, with its source; the legend, told as a legend; and how to visit the place legally and respectfully. There are no lurid details about real victims here: several of these sites are graves, temples or memorials.

口

1978-1979

The slit-mouthed woman: the panic of 1979

Gifu Prefecture, then all of Japan

What happened

In December 1978 a rumour began to spread in Gifu Prefecture about a masked woman who stopped children to ask whether she was pretty. The Gifu Nichinichi newspaper reported it on 26 January 1979, and by June the rumour had reached almost all of Japan. In cities such as Kōriyama and Hiratsuka police cars were sent out, and in others, such as Kushiro and Niiza, schools had children walk home in groups. In August, with the summer holidays, the rumour died out almost overnight.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 口裂け女 · Wikipedia (en): Kuchisake-onna

The legend

If you tell her she is not pretty, she attacks. If you say yes, she takes off her mask, shows a mouth that runs from ear to ear and asks again. In 1979 children passed remedies from school to school: answer “average”, throw her sweets, or say the word “pomade” three times.

How to visit

There is no place to visit: it is an urban legend. What is astonishing is how it spread across the whole country without the internet, from playground to playground, in a few months.

髪

1918 - today

Okiku, the doll whose hair grows

Mannen-ji temple, Iwamizawa (Hokkaidō)

What happened

In 1918 a 17-year-old boy, Eikichi Suzuki, bought a doll at a shop on Tanuki-kōji street in Sapporo for his three-year-old sister Kikuko. The girl died of a cold the following year, and the family kept the doll on the household altar. In 1938, when they moved to Sakhalin, they left it at Mannen-ji temple in Iwamizawa; after the war Eikichi asked the temple to keep it for good.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): お菊人形

The legend

The family noticed that the doll's hair, once short, now reached its shoulders, and they believed the girl's spirit had stayed in it. Ever since, people say the hair keeps growing, and Okiku became the most famous haunted doll in Japan.

How to visit

Mannen-ji is a working Buddhist temple in Iwamizawa, less than an hour by train from Sapporo. Ask at the temple whether the doll can be seen that day and do not photograph it without permission: for that family it was the memory of a little girl.

首

940 - today

Masakado's head mound, in the middle of the financial district

Shōmon-zuka, Ōtemachi (Chiyoda, Tokyo)

What happened

Taira no Masakado rebelled against the court in Kyoto, declared himself the “new emperor” and died in battle in 940. The mound in Ōtemachi, today surrounded by bank towers, is by tradition the resting place of his head. After the 1923 earthquake the Ministry of Finance put temporary offices on top of it, and several officials' deaths were blamed on the curse, including that of Finance Minister Hayami Seiji in 1926 (although he had nothing to do with the building work). In 1928 the ministry held a ceremony to appease his spirit. After the war, a plan to level the site was dropped after an accident with a bulldozer. It is a protected historic site of Tokyo and was renovated between November 2020 and April 2021.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 将門塚

The legend

Legend says Masakado's head, displayed in Kyoto, kept its eyes open and one night flew east in search of its body, until it fell here. Anyone who disrespects the mound or tries to move it brings misfortune on themselves: that is why no one has removed it in a thousand years.

How to visit

It is a few minutes' walk from Ōtemachi Station (Tokyo Metro). It is a place of worship: visit in silence and touch nothing. You will see little frog figures: in Japanese “frog” (kaeru) sounds the same as “to return”, as the head returned.

岩

1825 - today

Oiwa, the most famous ghost of Japanese theatre

Oiwa Inari Tamiya Jinja, Yotsuya (Shinjuku, Tokyo)

What happened

“Tōkaidō Yotsuya Kaidan”, by Tsuruya Nanboku IV, premiered in July 1825 at the Nakamura-za theatre in Edo, today's Tokyo. It tells the story of Oiwa, betrayed and poisoned on the orders of a husband who wants to marry someone else, who comes back as a ghost. The Tamiya Inari shrine in Yotsuya Samon-chō began as the household shrine of the Tamiya family, Oiwa's family, and its worship grew once the play became famous. By tradition, Oiwa's grave is at Myōgyō-ji temple in Sugamo.

Source: Wikipedia (en): Yotsuya Kaidan · Wikipedia (ja): 於岩稲荷田宮神社 (新宿区)

The legend

It is said that anyone who stages Yotsuya Kaidan without asking Oiwa's permission suffers accidents. So before every new production the lead actors and the director visit her grave to ask for her blessing. Those who look after her shrine tell another story: that the real Oiwa was a good wife and the ghost was invented by the theatre.

How to visit

The shrine is in Yotsuya Samon-chō, a few minutes' walk from Yotsuya-sanchōme Station (Marunouchi Line), and the small Yōun-ji temple, also dedicated to Oiwa, is very close. These are places of prayer: go in quietly and, if you like, leave a coin.

駅

8 January 2004

Kisaragi Station, the legend that was born live on the internet

Enshū Railway, Hamamatsu (Shizuoka)

What happened

Just after 11 p.m. on 8 January 2004, a user signing as “Hasumi” began posting live on the paranormal board of 2channel: her train would not stop and left her at a station that did not exist, “Kisaragi”. Readers worked out that she was on the Enshū Railway in Hamamatsu, and Saginomiya station became its unofficial place of pilgrimage. The photos that circulated later turned out to show real stations on other lines. In 2022 the film “Kisaragi Station” was released, and Enshū Railway itself sold a commemorative ticket that sold out in just over an hour.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): きさらぎ駅

The legend

There is no one at the station and nothing is open. Drums and bells can be heard in the distance, and a stranger offers to drive her into town. That is where her posts end. Since then, other users claim to have stepped off at stations that are on no map.

How to visit

The station does not exist. If you are a fan, the Enshū Railway leaves from Shin-Hamamatsu, about 1 h 30 by shinkansen from Shin-Osaka. Saginomiya is an ordinary station with locals going to work: no noise and no tripods on the platform.

犬

1949 - sealed

Inunaki Tunnel and the village that is not on the map

Old Inunaki Tunnel, between Miyawaka and Hisayama (Fukuoka)

What happened

The old Inunaki Tunnel opened in 1949 on the mountain road between Miyawaka and Hisayama, in Fukuoka. A new tunnel opened in 1975 and the old one fell out of use; it became an illegal dumping ground and a meeting point for biker gangs. Today its mouth is sealed with concrete blocks, and the old road has fences and cameras. It is the most famous “haunted” place in Kyūshū.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 犬鳴トンネル · Wikipedia (ja): 犬鳴村

The legend

The urban legend tells of an “Inunaki village” hidden beyond the tunnel, which appears on no map and where a sign warns that “the Constitution of Japan does not apply”. It is pure legend, but it inspired the horror film “Howling Village” (Inunaki-mura, 2020) by Takashi Shimizu.

How to visit

It cannot be visited: the tunnel is sealed and the old road is narrow, prone to rockfalls and closed to traffic. Going in is illegal and dangerous. The new road and tunnel are in normal use; if you want the chill, watch the film instead.

柱

1607-1611

The human pillar of Matsue Castle

Matsue Castle (Shimane)

What happened

Horio Yoshiharu built Matsue Castle between 1607 and 1611. Its keep is one of the twelve original keeps left in Japan and has been a National Treasure since 2015. The writer Lafcadio Hearn, who lived in Matsue, recorded the castle's “hitobashira” (human pillar) legend in an 1894 book. There is no evidence that it happened: it is a legend, and it has always been told as one.

Source: Wikipedia (en): Matsue Castle · Wikipedia (ja): 人柱

The legend

The story goes that the stone wall of the keep kept collapsing, and that to make it hold, a young woman taken from the circle of the Bon dance was buried beneath it. Hearn wrote that from then on no girl danced in the streets of Matsue, because the hill would shake and the castle tremble. Another version tells of an old mendicant monk whose shakuhachi flute can be heard on rainy evenings.

How to visit

From Osaka: JR to Okayama and the Yakumo limited express to Matsue (about 3 h 30 in total), or a night bus. The castle keep is open inside; Lafcadio Hearn's house and museum are a few minutes away, and the town runs evening ghost-story walks.

狐

1884 - 1970s

Kokkuri-san, the spirit game that frightened schools

Born in Shimoda (Izu), spread across Japan

What happened

According to the most widely cited version, in 1884 American sailors who came ashore at Shimoda showed local people “table-turning”, a spiritualist game fashionable in their country. In Japan the table was improvised from a rice tub on three bamboo canes, and because it tipped — “kokkuri, kokkuri” — the game took that name, later written with the characters for fox, dog and tanuki. The philosopher Inoue Enryō studied it in his book “Yōkai gendan” and concluded that it was moved by the players' own expectations and muscles. In the 1970s it came back strongly thanks to the manga “Ushiro no Hyakutarō” by Jirō Tsunoda.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): コックリさん

The legend

Today it is played with a sheet showing the Japanese syllables, a drawn torii and a ten-yen coin on which everyone rests a finger. Players summon Kokkuri-san, a fox spirit, who moves the coin to answer. The golden rule: never let go of the coin before saying goodbye, or the spirit stays with you.

How to visit

It is not a place. In Japan some teachers explicitly ask pupils not to play and even warn parents, because children end up frightened. If you are curious about the history, Tokyo has the Tetsugakudō, the “philosophy park” founded by Inoue Enryō, the man who tried to explain ghosts with reason.

花

Since around 1950

Hanako-san, the girl in the school toilet

Schools all over Japan

What happened

It is the best-known school legend in Japan. Its origin is traced to around 1950, in a story called “the third Hanako-san”. By the 1980s children all over the country were telling it, and in the 1990s it moved into films, manga and anime.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): トイレの花子さん

The legend

In the third-floor toilets of the school, you knock three times on the door of the third stall and ask: “Hanako-san, are you there?”. A faint voice answers “yes”… and, depending on who tells it, a girl with a short bob and a red skirt opens the door.

How to visit

You cannot visit: Japanese schools are not open to the public. But almost any Japanese person knows the story: ask whoever is with you what “their” Hanako-san was like.

線

Modern legend

Teke Teke, the sound that follows you at night

Urban legend from all over Japan

What happened

Teke Teke is a modern urban legend, included in Japanese collections of urban legends (for example “Saikyō no toshi densetsu”, 2007) and adapted for film. No real case is known to lie behind it.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): テケテケ

The legend

It is the spirit of a woman missing the lower half of her body who moves on her arms at great speed, making a “teke teke, teke teke” sound. The story places her beside a railway line on a winter night in northern Japan. If you hear her behind you, they say, running is no use.

How to visit

There is no place to visit. It is one of those stories told in the dark on school trips, and it still works even though nobody believes it.

八

26 August 2008

Hachishakusama, the woman 2.4 metres tall

Born on the 2channel forum

What happened

The story was posted on 26 August 2008 on the paranormal board of 2channel, in a thread of scary stories. It is therefore an internet creation, but it has been retold so often that many Japanese people now know it as if it were an ancient yokai.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 八尺様

The legend

In a country village a very tall woman appears, eight shaku tall (about 240 cm), in a white dress and a hat, laughing with a strange “po, po, po”. If she fixes on a child, she pursues him. To protect him, the family shuts him in a room with salt and charms for a night and, at dawn, drives him out of the village surrounded by his relatives' cars.

How to visit

It is not a real place. If you travel through rural Japan you will see tall hedges and quiet houses; none of them has a Hachishakusama, but after reading this you will look at hedges differently.

丑

Traditional ritual

Ushi no koku mairi, the 2 a.m. curse

Kifune Shrine, north of Kyoto

What happened

Ushi no koku mairi is a traditional cursing ritual: at the “hour of the ox” (between 1 and 3 a.m.), dressed in white and wearing an iron trivet with three candles on the head, the person nails a straw doll to the sacred tree of a shrine. It is linked to Kifune because, by tradition, its god descended in the year, month, day and hour of the ox. Noh theatre put it on stage in the play “Kanawa”. Today, nailing anything to a shrine tree can be prosecuted as criminal damage.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 丑の刻参り · Kifune Jinja (web oficial)

The legend

The oldest version is that of Hashihime of Uji: a woman maddened by jealousy prayed at Kifune to become a demon, and the god told her to immerse herself in the Uji river for twenty-one days. It is said that if someone sees you performing the ritual, it loses its power… and the curse turns back on you.

How to visit

Today Kifune is a shrine of water and of love, beautiful with its stairway of red lanterns. From Osaka: Keihan to Demachiyanagi, Eizan train to Kibuneguchi and a bus or a half-hour walk (about 2 hours in total). Come in daylight, to read your fortune in the water (see Mysterious places).

耳

1597

Mimizuka, the memorial to Japan’s 16th-century war in Korea

Higashiyama, Kyoto (next to Toyokuni Shrine)

What happened

This is not a legend: it is history. During the invasions of Korea ordered by Toyotomi Hideyoshi (1592-1598), Japanese soldiers cut the noses from the dead to prove how many they had killed. In 1597 those of tens of thousands of people, soldiers and civilians, were buried in this mound: sources cite at least 38,000 Koreans and more than 30,000 Chinese. It was first called the “nose mound”; the name Mimizuka, “ear mound”, came later.

Source: Wikipedia (en): Mimizuka

The legend

There is no legend here, and that is as it should be. In 1990 a Korean Buddhist monk held a ceremony for the victims, and in 1997, on the 400th anniversary, people of all nationalities and faiths gathered here to remember them.

How to visit

It is a 5-minute walk from the Kyoto National Museum, near Shichijō station (Keihan), about 50 minutes from Osaka. It is a place of remembrance: visit in silence, without group photos or laughter, and take time to read the explanatory sign in front of it.

千

Edo period (from 1615)

Sennichimae, the nightlife district that was a cemetery and an execution ground

Namba, Osaka (next to Dōtonbori)

What happened

Where today there are theatres, comedy halls and restaurants, there was once one of Osaka's great cemeteries. Around 1615 the Sennichi cemetery was opened here, with a crematorium and an execution ground beside it. In the Meiji era the execution ground was abolished and the cemetery was moved to Abeno; at first the land was so hard to sell that the city helped those who bought it. The name comes from the “thousand-day” prayers (sennichi nenbutsu) of the Hōzen-ji and Chikurin-ji temples.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 千日前

The legend

The district's own history notes that the cemetery and the execution ground gave rise to many rumours and ghost stories. The living tradition is a different one: the Mizukake Fudō of Hōzen-ji, a statue people splash with water to make a wish, now completely covered in moss.

How to visit

A 5-minute walk from Namba station. Hōzen-ji sits in a stone-paved alley between Dōtonbori and Sennichimae: splash the Fudō with the ladle, put your hands together and make your wish in silence. Close by is Sennichimae Dōguyasuji, the kitchenware street.

雷

903-947

Sugawara no Michizane: the curse that turned a man into a god

Kitano Tenmangū, Kyoto

What happened

Sugawara no Michizane was a scholar and court minister. The victim of a plot, in 901 he was exiled to Dazaifu, in Kyūshū, where he died in 903. In the following years several of his rivals and members of the imperial family died, there were epidemics and floods, and in 930 lightning struck the Seiryōden hall of the imperial palace. The court blamed his vengeful spirit and, to calm it, made him a god: Kitano Tenmangū shrine was founded in 947.

Source: Wikipedia (en): Sugawara no Michizane · Kitano Tenmangū (web oficial)

The legend

Michizane is the most famous onryō (vengeful spirit) in Japanese history: the thunder god who punished those who betrayed him. Today, curiously, people pray to him to pass exams, because in life he was the wisest man of his time. The stone oxen of the shrine recall that, by tradition, it was an ox that chose where he would be buried, by stopping and refusing to go on.

How to visit

From Osaka: train to Kyoto and bus 50 or 101 to Kitano Tenmangū-mae (about 1 h 30 in total). In February its roughly 1,500 plum trees bloom. Rub the head of an ox for luck, as students do.

石

March 2022

Sesshō-seki, the killing stone… that split in 2022

Nasu Yumoto (Tochigi)

What happened

In the volcanic mountains of Nasu, an area of hot springs and sulphur smells, there is a large rock wrapped in sacred ropes: the Sesshō-seki, “the killing stone”. In Japan the name has been given to rocks in areas with dangerous volcanic gases, and this is the most famous one. On 5 March 2022 it was reported that it had split in two, probably through natural weathering, and the news went round the world. On 26 March priests held a ceremony there to appease its spirit.

Source: Wikipedia (en): Sessho-seki

The legend

Legend says the rock is the body of Tamamo-no-Mae, a beautiful court lady who was really a nine-tailed fox. Unmasked and struck down by a warrior, she turned into this stone, which killed anyone who touched it, until the monk Gennō exorcised it. When it split in 2022, many people on social media wondered whether the fox had been set free.

How to visit

It is far from Osaka: shinkansen to Tokyo and on to Nasushiobara, then a bus to Nasu Yumoto Onsen (about 5 hours). The stone is next to a signposted path: respect the ropes and the gas warnings.

飴

Around 1599 - today

The candy of the ghost who raised her child

Minatoya, Matsubara street (Higashiyama, Kyoto)

What happened

On Matsubara street, a few steps from Rokudō Chinnō-ji temple, the small Minatoya shop still sells “yūrei kosodate ame”, the candy of the ghost who raised her child. According to the shop's own sign, that child became a monk and died in 1666 at the age of 68, which would place the story around 1599. It used to be a syrup; today it is hard amber-coloured candy.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 子育て幽霊

The legend

Every night a pale woman bought candy with a single coin. When her coins ran out, the shopkeeper followed her to a cemetery and heard a baby crying under the ground: the woman had died while expecting a child, the baby had been born in the grave, and she, now a ghost, had been feeding it with candy. The child was brought out alive.

How to visit

About a 10-minute walk from Kiyomizu-Gojō station (Keihan), some 50 minutes from Osaka. Combine it with Rokudō Chinnō-ji, the temple of the well to the other world (File no. 2). The shop is small and family-run: buy, say thank you and make room for the next person.
