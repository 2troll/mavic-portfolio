Incredible sites, most of them a day trip from Osaka

墓

World Heritage Site since 2004

Okunoin, the forest cemetery of more than 200,000 graves

Mount Kōya (Wakayama)

What is known

Okunoin is the largest cemetery in Japan: more than 200,000 graves and memorials beneath centuries-old cedars, along some 2 km of stone path. At the end lies the mausoleum of Kūkai (Kōbō Daishi), the monk who founded Kōya-san in the 9th century. Feudal lords who were enemies in life, ordinary families and modern companies share the same forest.

Source: Kōyasan Shingon-shū Sōhonzan Kongōbu-ji (web oficial) · Wikipedia (en): Okunoin

The legend

The faithful believe Kūkai did not die in 835 but remains in eternal meditation, waiting for the future Buddha. That is why monks bring him food twice a day, every day, as they have for almost twelve centuries.

Getting there from Osaka

From Namba: Nankai Kōya Line to Gokurakubashi, cable car and bus (about 2 hours). Beyond Gobyō-bashi bridge photography is not allowed. It is magical at dawn or at night, on the walks led by the monks themselves.

水

Mountain valley

Kifune Shrine, home of the god of water

Kibune, north of Kyoto

What is known

Kifune lies in a narrow valley of cedars and streams north of Kyoto and is dedicated to Takaokami, the god of water and rain. Its stone stairway lined with red lanterns is one of the most famous images of Kyoto. It has three shrines along the river: the main shrine, the Yui-no-yashiro and the Okumiya.

Source: Kifune Jinja (web oficial) · Wikipedia (ja): 丑の刻参り

The legend

A thousand years ago the poet Izumi Shikibu prayed at the Yui-no-yashiro to win back her husband's love, and it is said she succeeded. Kifune is also the shrine linked to ushi no koku mairi, the small-hours cursing ritual (see True horror). And its water reads your fortune with the “mizu-mikuji”: a blank slip of paper that reveals its message when wet.

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: Keihan to Demachiyanagi, Eizan train to Kibuneguchi and a bus or a half-hour walk (about 2 hours). Combine it with Kurama over the mountain trail. In summer it is noticeably cooler than Kyoto.

天

Mountain temple

Kurama-dera, the temple of the being who came from Venus

Mount Kurama, north of Kyoto

What is known

Kurama-dera is a mountain temple an hour from central Kyoto that venerates its own trinity, Sonten, which includes Maōson, the great king of spirits. Between Kurama and Kifune runs a path where cedar roots break out of the ground like snakes, the Kinone-michi, and halfway along stands the small Sōjōbō hall, dedicated to the king of the tengu.

Source: Kurama-dera (web oficial)

The legend

According to the temple's own teaching, Maōson came down to Earth from Venus 6.5 million years ago. And in this same forest, at night, the king of the tengu taught the young Yoshitsune to fight with the sword (see File no. 2).

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: Keihan to Demachiyanagi and the Eizan train to Kurama (about 1 h 45). Go up by cable car or on foot; the Kurama-Kifune crossing over the mountain takes about 2 hours with many steps. Wear sturdy shoes.

聖

Restored in 1678

Hōzan-ji, the temple built against the cliffs of Mount Ikoma

Mount Ikoma (Nara), on the border with Osaka

What is known

Hōzan-ji, known as Ikoma Shōten, is built against the rock face of Mount Ikoma. Its effective founder was the monk Tankai, who restored it in 1678. It venerates Fudō Myōō and Kangiten (Shōten), a deity of Indian origin much prayed to by Osaka's merchants. Above the temple, halfway up the cliff, opens the Hannya-kutsu cave.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 宝山寺

The legend

Tradition says Mount Ikoma was opened as a place of ascetic training by the legendary En no Gyōja, who placed the wisdom sutras in the Hannya-kutsu cave, and that Kūkai trained here too. With its rows of Jizō, its stone lanterns and its stairways in the mist, it is one of the most atmospheric places in Kansai.

Getting there from Osaka

From Namba: Kintetsu Nara Line to Ikoma (about 25 minutes) and the Ikoma cable car (Umeyashiki station is closer to the temple than Hōzanji station). It is a very active temple: go in quietly and do not photograph people at prayer.

猿

Founded in 960

Yasaka Kōshindō, the temple of the tied-up monkeys

Higashiyama, Kyoto (by the Yasaka pagoda)

What is known

This tiny temple, founded in 960 according to its tradition, is one of the three great Kōshin temples of Japan. It is covered in thousands of colourful cloth balls: the “kukurizaru”, monkeys with their hands and feet tied, messengers of its deity.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 八坂庚申堂 · Wikipedia (en): Kōshin

The legend

Kōshin belief holds that three worms live in the body and, every sixty days, on Kōshin night, slip out while you sleep to report your faults to heaven. That is why people stayed awake that night. The tied monkey stands for desire held in check: you hang one with a written wish and, in exchange, give up one indulgence.

Getting there from Osaka

A 15-minute walk from Gion-Shijō station (Keihan), about 50 minutes from Osaka. It is small and much photographed: go early, let people who come to pray go first and do not climb on anything for a photo.

鳥

Open 24 hours

Fushimi Inari at night: ten thousand torii and no one else

Fushimi, south Kyoto

What is known

Fushimi Inari Taisha is the head shrine of some 30,000 Inari shrines in Japan and has around ten thousand torii gates climbing to the top of Mount Inari (233 m). It never closes: at night the paths are lit by lanterns and empty out.

Source: Fushimi Inari Taisha (web oficial) · Wikipedia (en): Fushimi Inari-taisha

The legend

At night the stone foxes seem to watch anyone climbing alone (their legends are in File no. 2). At the inner shrine stand the “omokaru-ishi”, two stone lanterns with a ball on top: make a wish and lift the ball; if it feels lighter than you expected, your wish will come true.

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: Keihan from Yodoyabashi to Fushimi-Inari (about 50 minutes), or JR via Kyoto to Inari. Climbing to the top at night takes about 2 hours there and back: bring a torch, water and comfortable shoes, and never leave the path.

仏

Since 811

Adashino Nenbutsu-ji, the eight thousand statues of the forgotten

Saga-Toriimoto, Arashiyama (Kyoto)

What is known

From the Heian period Adashino was a burial ground where bodies were left in the open. By tradition, in 811 Kūkai buried the abandoned remains here and founded a temple, and later Hōnen opened a place of prayer. The roughly 8,000 stone statues and stupas that fill the courtyard were found in the surrounding area and date from the Heian to the Edo period.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 化野念仏寺

The legend

In classical Japanese poetry, “Adashino” stands for how fragile life is. Every year, at Jizō-bon in late August (the 23rd and 24th), thousands of candles are lit among the statues in the Sentō kuyō, the offering of a thousand lights, for the souls no one remembers.

Getting there from Osaka

From JR Saga-Arashiyama station, about a 30-minute walk along the old Saga-Toriimoto street, or a Kyoto Bus to Toriimoto (about 1 h 15 from Osaka). Next to it is a small bamboo grove far quieter than the famous one. Follow the signs: there are areas where photos are not allowed.

星

9th-century tradition

Hoshida Myōken, the shrine of the star that fell from the sky

Hoshida, Katano (Osaka)

What is known

In the hills of Katano, east of Osaka, the Hoshida Myōken-gū shrine (officially Komatsu-jinja) venerates as its sacred body a large rock, the Yōgō-seki, also called the “Weaver Stone”. Tanabata rites have been held here for centuries; the illustrated guide “Kawachi meisho zue” already recorded it in 1801.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 星田妙見宮

The legend

Tradition says that between 810 and 823, while Kūkai was reciting secret rites in a cave of the nearby Shishikutsu-ji temple, seven stars fell from the sky in three places in Katano, and he consecrated the one that fell on this hill. Today the story is often told as a meteorite fall, but no study has confirmed the rock came from space: it is a tradition, tied to the Tanabata legend of the Weaver and the Herdsman parted by the Milky Way.

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: JR Gakkentoshi Line to Hoshida and about a 20-minute walk (about 50 minutes in total from the city centre). Nearby is Hoshida Park, with the Hoshi no Buranko, a 280 m suspension bridge over the forest. It is hiking country: wear comfortable shoes.

滝

One of Japan’s 100 best waterfalls

Minoo Falls and the ascetic who had two demons as servants

Minoo, north of Osaka

What is known

Minoo Falls drops 33 metres in a maple forest less than an hour from central Osaka, and is among Japan's hundred finest waterfalls. Since ancient times the valley was a training ground for shugendō, the way of the mountain ascetics, and on the path stands Ryūan-ji temple, linked by tradition to En no Gyōja.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 箕面滝

The legend

En no Gyōja (7th century) is Japan's great legendary ascetic: it is said he could fly, walk on water and had two demons, Zenki and Goki, as servants. According to the valley's tradition, he trained beneath this waterfall.

Getting there from Osaka

From Umeda: Hankyu to Minoo (about 30 minutes) and about 35 minutes on foot along the river path (2.8 km, almost flat). In autumn it fills with red maples. Feeding the valley's monkeys is forbidden.

蛇

One of the oldest shrines in Japan

Ōmiwa Shrine, the shrine with no main hall: the god is the mountain

Sakurai (Nara)

What is known

Ōmiwa Shrine has no main hall: people pray directly to the mountain behind it, Mount Miwa, which is itself the sacred body of the god. It is one of the oldest forms of worship in Japan. You can climb the mountain, but only after registering at Sai Shrine, wearing a sash around your neck, with no photos and no eating on the way up.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 大神神社 · Ōmiwa Jinja (web oficial)

The legend

The mountain's god, Ōmononushi, is considered a serpent god. The “Kojiki” tells that he visited a young woman at night without saying who he was; she tied a thread to his clothes and, following it in the morning, the thread led her to the shrine on Mount Miwa.

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: Kintetsu to Sakurai and the JR Man'yō Mahoroba Line to Miwa, a 10-minute walk (about 1 h 20 in total). Climbing the mountain takes about 2-3 hours there and back; rules, hours and the offering are on the official website.

宝

One of the “three wonders of Japan”

Ishi-no-Hōden, the 500-tonne rock that seems to float

Ōshiko Shrine, Takasago (Hyōgo)

What is known

At Ōshiko Shrine stands a carved stone block 6.4 m wide, 5.7 m high and 7.2 m deep, weighing more than 500 tonnes. It is joined to the bedrock only by a pillar at the centre of its base, which lies in shadow, so it seems to float on the water of the pond around it. Nobody knows who carved it, when or why; the most accepted theory is that it was an unfinished burial chamber. It has been a national historic site since 2014.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 石の宝殿

The legend

For the shrine, the rock is the work of two gods who tried to build a stone palace in a single night and never finished it. Together with the Shiogama of Shiogama Shrine and the heavenly spear of Kirishima, it forms the “three wonders of Japan” (Nihon sanki).

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: JR Kobe Line to Hōden station (about 1 hour; depending on the train, change at Kakogawa) and about a 25-minute walk. You can walk around the rock and climb the hill behind it to see it from above.

岩

7th century

The mysterious stones of Asuka and the Masuda no Iwafune

Kashihara and Asuka (Nara)

What is known

Asuka was the capital of Japan in the 7th century, and its hills are full of carved stones whose purpose nobody knows. The largest is the Masuda no Iwafune in Kashihara: a block of about 11 by 8 m and 4.7 m high, with a trench and two 1.6 m square holes cut into the top. Depending on the source, it is estimated at anything from 160 to more than 500 tonnes. The most accepted theory is that it was a burial chamber abandoned when it cracked during the work.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 益田岩船

The legend

It has been called a star observatory, the base of a stele written by Kūkai, or a watchtower. In the same area are the turtle stone (Kameishi), the monkey stones (Saruishi) and the Ishibutai, a tomb of huge stones attributed to Soga no Umako.

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka-Abenobashi: Kintetsu to Okadera (about 50 minutes) and about 20 minutes on foot; the last 5 are a steep slope with a rope, dangerous in the rain. Asuka is best explored by rented bicycle.

峰

World Heritage Site since 2004

Ōminesan, the sacred mountain women still may not enter

Sanjōgatake, Dorogawa (Tenkawa, Nara)

What is known

Mount Sanjōgatake, in the Ōmine range, is the cradle of shugendō, opened by tradition by En no Gyōja at the end of the 7th century. Since 2004 its temples and trails have been part of the World Heritage Site “Sacred Sites and Pilgrimage Routes in the Kii Mountain Range”. It is one of the few places in Japan that still bans women (nyonin kinsei): large gates on the trail say so. Neighbouring Mount Inamuragatake has been open to everyone since 1960.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 大峰山

The legend

Yamabushi ascetics still perform tests of courage here such as the “Nishi no nozoki”: holding you by a rope, they lean you head-first over a precipice while asking whether you will be a good son and work honestly.

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: Kintetsu to Shimoichiguchi and a bus to Dorogawa Onsen (about 2 h 30). The summit temple only opens from early May to late September. This is real mountain terrain, with chains and steep slopes. Women can visit Dorogawa and climb Inamuragatake; respect the marked boundaries.

橋

Rebuilt every 3 years

Kazurabashi, the vine bridge of the Iya Valley

Nishi-Iya, Miyoshi (Tokushima)

What is known

In the Iya Valley, one of the most remote in Japan, a vine bridge 45 m long and 2 m wide crosses the river 14 m up. It is rebuilt every three years with shirakuchi-kazura vines and is an Important Tangible Folk Cultural Property of Japan. The planks are spaced apart: you can see the river between your feet.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 祖谷のかずら橋

The legend

Legend says it was built by Heike samurai who fled into these mountains after losing the war in the 12th century: a vine bridge could be cut in a moment if enemies came. Nobody knows whether it is true, but the valley remains proud of the story.

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: train to Okayama, Nanpū limited express to Ōboke and a bus (about 4 hours). Crossing costs ¥550 (adults, 2022 price). Better over two days: the valley has farmhouse stays.

黄

From the “Kojiki” (712)

Yomotsu Hirasaka, the entrance to the land of the dead

Higashi-Izumo, Matsue (Shimane)

What is known

In Higashi-Izumo, on the outskirts of Matsue, there is a place tradition identifies with Yomotsu Hirasaka, the slope leading to Yomi, the land of the dead in the “Kojiki” (712). There are large rocks, among them the so-called “thousand-man rock”, and a stone monument raised in 1940. Nearby is Iya Shrine, dedicated to the goddess Izanami.

Source: Wikipedia (ja): 黄泉比良坂

The legend

Izanagi went down to the land of the dead to find his wife Izanami; she asked him not to look at her, he looked and saw what she had become. He fled, chased by the creatures of Yomi, and on the way out sealed the slope with a rock that would take a thousand men to move. There, one on each side of the stone, husband and wife said goodbye for ever.

Getting there from Osaka

Osaka to Matsue takes about 3 h 30 (see Matsue Castle in True horror), then a JR train to Iya and about a 25-minute walk. It is a small, quiet place in the woods: go in daylight and combine it with Matsue Castle.

海

Found in 1986

The Yonaguni underwater monument: ruins or rock?

Yonaguni Island (Okinawa), Japan’s westernmost island

What is known

In 1986, while looking for hammerhead sharks, the diver Kihachirō Aratake saw beneath the Arakawabana cliff, down to 26 m deep, rock terraces with steps and almost right angles. The geologist Masaaki Kimura of the University of the Ryukyus argues that it was built by humans. Other geologists, such as Robert Schoch, think it is sandstone that naturally breaks along straight planes, and a 2019 study explained it by erosion. Neither the Agency for Cultural Affairs nor Okinawa Prefecture recognises it as an archaeological site.

Source: Wikipedia (en): Yonaguni Monument

The legend

For its supporters it is the “Japanese Atlantis”: a pyramid, roads and a stadium swallowed by the sea. To be honest about it: there is no archaeological evidence today that anyone built it, but diving over those “giant stairs” is just as impressive.

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: fly to Naha or Ishigaki and on to Yonaguni. It is seen by diving, with strong currents, so only with a dive centre and experience. It is the farthest place in this file, and the only one where the question is still open.

家

Since 1616

Kunōzan Tōshōgū: where does Tokugawa Ieyasu really rest?

Kunōzan, Shizuoka

What is known

Tokugawa Ieyasu, the shōgun who unified Japan, died in 1616 in Sunpu (today's Shizuoka). His will, recorded in the diary of the monk Sūden, asked for his body to be buried at Kunōzan, his funeral to be held at Zōjō-ji in Edo and, after the first anniversary, a small shrine to be built at Nikkō. That is what happened: the Kunōzan shrine, reached by 1,159 steps above the sea, has his mausoleum behind the main hall, a National Treasure since 2010. In 1617 his cult was moved to Nikkō, which would become the great Tōshōgū.

Source: Kunōzan Tōshōgū (web oficial) · Wikipedia (ja): 久能山東照宮 · Tabi-mag: ¿dónde está el cuerpo de Tokugawa Ieyasu?

The legend

Ever since there has been a debate: did his body travel to Nikkō, or only his spirit? Nikkō has his official tomb; Kunōzan maintains that his remains were buried here, and today it is considered more likely that they never left this mountain: the body was buried without cremation and moving it would have been very difficult. There is no final proof: Japan's most powerful shōgun has two tombs and a mystery.

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: shinkansen to Shizuoka (about 2 hours), a bus to Nihondaira and a ropeway of about 5 minutes to the shrine; or a bus to the foot of the mountain and the 1,159 steps. From the Nihondaira viewpoint, on a clear day, you can see Mount Fuji.

火

Since 806

Mount Misen and the flame that has burned since 806

Miyajima (Hiroshima)

What is known

On Mount Misen (535 m), Miyajima's sacred mountain, Daishō-in temple keeps the “kiezu no hi”, the flame that never goes out: by tradition Kūkai lit it in 806, and monks have kept it alive ever since. On 1 August 1964 it was used to light the Flame of Peace in Hiroshima's Peace Memorial Park.

Source: Wikipedia (en): Mount Misen · Agencia de Turismo de Japón: «Misen, la llama que no se apaga»

The legend

The flame is the first of the “seven wonders of Misen”. The others are the plum tree that grew from Kūkai's staff, the Mandara rock, the tide rock (a hollow 500 m up whose water level rises and falls with the tide), the sound of wooden clappers heard with no one striking them, the Shigure-zakura cherry tree and the cedar of the dragon lights. It is also said that water boiled over the flame cures illness.

Getting there from Osaka

From Osaka: shinkansen to Hiroshima (about 1 h 30), JR to Miyajimaguchi and a 10-minute ferry; on the island, the ropeway and about 30 minutes on foot to the summit. Hiroshima and Miyajima fit into a single day from Osaka if you start early.
